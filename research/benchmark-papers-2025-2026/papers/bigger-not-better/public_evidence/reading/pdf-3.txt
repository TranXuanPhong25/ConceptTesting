         5      Discussion

        The results show that model size alone is not a reliable indicator of repair effectiveness in the
        evaluated Python bug-fixing setting. Qwen1.5, the smallest model in the study, achieved the
        strongest repair correctness while also requiring the lowest inference time and GPU energy
        consumption. In contrast, larger models, such as StarCoder15, incurred substantially higher
        runtime and energy costs without corresponding gains in correctness. This suggests that
        scaling model size does not automatically improve correctness; there are energy trade-offs in
        LLM-based repair.
S. F. A. Naqvi and H. Anwar                                                                        34:17


   One explanation is the block-level and format-constrained nature of the repair task. Each
model was asked to generate a complete replacement for a function or class and to output
only executable Python code. The exploratory failure analysis suggests that several failures
were caused not only by incorrect repair logic, but also by output-structure problems such as
extra text, duplicated code, incomplete blocks, or code outside the expected structure. In
such a setting, concise, structurally compliant outputs may be more useful than longer or
more complex outputs.
   The project and category-level results further indicate that repair success depends on
the characteristics of the underlying bugs. The evaluated web-related bugs were solved
more consistently across models, whereas DevTool, Data, and Automation tasks were more
challenging. This suggests that future studies should examine bug characteristics more
explicitly, including locality, dependency structure, and whether the repair requires a broader
repository context.
   For researchers, these findings reinforce the need to report inference time and GPU energy
alongside repair correctness. For practitioners, the results suggest that smaller open-source
code LLMs should not be dismissed solely because of their size, especially in local or repeated
debugging workflows. However, these implications are limited to the evaluated models,
prompt, benchmark, and hardware setup, and should not be interpreted as a general claim
that smaller models are always preferable.


 6     Threats to Validity
Construct validity. Repair correctness is measured using execution-based testing: a gener-
ated repair is considered correct if it passes the bug-specific test suite. This is common in
automated program repair, but passing the available tests does not guarantee semantic equi-
valence to the developer patch or correctness for all inputs. In addition, this study measures
GPU energy during model inference using NVML. The reported values therefore represent
GPU-side repair-generation energy, not full-system or end-to-end pipeline energy; CPU,
memory, disk, cooling, repository setup, patch insertion, and test execution are excluded.
The repair task is also limited to function- or class-level replacement, so bugs requiring
multi-location edits, new imports outside the extracted block, configuration changes, or
broader repository-level reasoning are not captured.

Internal validity. Energy and runtime measurements can be affected by GPU state, thermal
behavior, background processes, caching, and scheduling effects. To reduce these threats, all
experiments were executed on the same dedicated GPU node with an identical configuration,
a fixed delay between repeated runs, and baseline idle energy subtraction. Full-system
energy could not be measured because CPU and host-level energy profiling were restricted
in the HPC environment. Model-output variability is another threat. We did not apply
task-specific or model-specific decoding tuning and relied on the default generation behavior
of the inference library/model configuration. Random seeds were not fixed; instead, each
bug-model pair was executed 10 times, and results were aggregated across attempts. Invalid
or non-compliant outputs, such as Markdown, explanations, incomplete code, syntax errors,
or outputs with an unexpected structure, were counted as unsuccessful repairs and were not
manually edited.

External validity. The study initially selected 40 Python bugs from BugsInPy, of which 35
were retained for the balanced aggregate comparison across all six models due to complete
and consistent run coverage. The six evaluated open-source code-oriented LLMs range


                                                                                                  ESEM 2026
34:18   Performance and Sustainability Trade-Offs in LLMs for Python Bug-Fixing Tasks


        from 1.5B to 15B parameters and were executed on a single hardware configuration: an
        NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU with 32 GB of memory, 4 CPU cores, and 128 GB of RAM.
        Results may not generalize to proprietary models, larger or newer models, quantized variants,
        tool-augmented repair systems, other programming languages, other benchmarks, or different
        hardware. Results may also differ under alternative prompt designs, decoding configurations,
        or model-specific tuning. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence for the
        evaluated models, tasks, prompts, and hardware setup. In particular, our conclusions should
        not be generalized to automated program repair scenarios requiring repository-level reasoning,
        cross-file modifications, or broader contextual understanding.

        Conclusion validity. Repeated runs, statistical tests, and effect sizes are used to support
        comparisons across models. However, the benchmark size is limited, and multiple pairwise
        comparisons may increase the risk of false positives; therefore, statistical results are interpreted
        together with descriptive trends and effect sizes. The qualitative failure analysis is exploratory
        and is used to support the interpretation of quantitative results rather than to provide a
        validated taxonomy of LLM repair failures.

         7     Conclusion
        This study presents an empirical evaluation of open-source LLMs for localized Python
        bug-fixing tasks, where each repair replaces a single extracted function or class. Using
        the BugsInPy benchmark, models ranging from 1.5B to 15B parameters were evaluated
        under controlled conditions. The results show that increasing model size does not con-
        sistently improve performance for the localized repair scenarios investigated in this study.
        In particular, the smallest model (Qwen 1.5B) achieved the highest overall performance
        while consuming significantly less energy. In contrast, larger models incurred substantially
        higher computational costs without delivering proportional gains in correctness, indicating
        diminishing returns from scaling. The qualitative analysis further suggests that output
        characteristics, such as conciseness and structural correctness, can affect repair success in the
        evaluated block-level setting. In the inspected cases, some medium-sized models produced
        longer or more structurally complex outputs, which were more susceptible to formatting or
        structural failures. These findings highlight an important trade-off between performance and
        sustainability in AI-assisted software engineering. Rather than defaulting to larger models,
        practitioners should select models based on task requirements, reliability constraints, and
        resource efficiency.

        Future Work
        This study can be extended in several directions. First, to include a broader range of models,
        including larger-scale and proprietary systems, to better understand how model size and
        architectural differences influence both performance and energy consumption. In addition,
        exploring alternative prompting strategies, such as few-shot and instruction-based prompting,
        may provide further insight into how input design affects both correctness and computational
        efficiency. Second, the evaluation can be expanded to cover additional programming languages
        and more diverse software systems, enabling analysis of whether the observed trends generalize
        beyond Python-based bug-fixing tasks. Incorporating a larger set of bugs, including those
        currently excluded due to dependency or execution constraints, would further improve the
        representativeness of the results. Finally, future studies can extend energy measurement
        beyond GPU inference to capture full-system consumption, including CPU, memory, and
        the complete execution pipeline.
