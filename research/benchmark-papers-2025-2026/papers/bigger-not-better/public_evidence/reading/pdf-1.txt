      Abstract

Background. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in software engineering tasks
such as code generation, bug detection, and program repair. However, their use introduces inference-
time costs that are rarely considered together with functional performance, especially in debugging
and repair workflows where models may be invoked repeatedly.

Aim. This study evaluates the correctness-energy trade-offs of LLMs across model scales in Python
bug-fixing tasks. We examine how model size relates to code correctness, inference time, and GPU
energy consumption.

Method. We conduct a controlled empirical evaluation of six open-source code-oriented LLMs,
ranging from 1.5B to 15B parameters, on 40 real-world Python bugs from the BugsInPy benchmark.
Each model is executed multiple times per task under a consistent hardware and prompting setup.
Repair performance is measured using Pass@k and test-suite outcomes, while computational cost is
measured using inference time and GPU energy consumption. We also analyze generated outputs to
identify recurring failure patterns.

Results. Increasing model size substantially raised computational cost without consistently improv-
ing correctness. The smallest model, Qwen 1.5B, achieved the best overall performance, with a
Pass@1 score of 43% and an average runtime of 38.7 seconds. In contrast, StarCoder 15B achieved a
Pass@1 score of 23%, required 96.7 seconds, and consumed approximately 7 times more energy per
inference while solving fewer tasks. Higher success rates were also observed in web-related projects,
suggesting that structurally localized bugs are more effectively handled by current models.

Conclusion. On the evaluated localized Python bug-fixing tasks, smaller LLMs offered a more
favorable trade-off between correctness, inference time, and energy consumption. These results
suggest that scaling model size does not automatically lead to more efficient LLM-based repair, and
that inference cost should be considered alongside functional correctness when comparing models.

        1     Introduction

       Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in software engineering tasks, including
       code generation, bug detection, automated debugging, and program repair [12]. Code-oriented
       LLMs can generate patches, complete missing code, and assist developers in reasoning about
       faulty program behavior. As these models become more accessible through local deployment
       and integrated development tools, they are likely to be invoked repeatedly during software
       development workflows.
           This growing use of LLMs raises an important sustainability concern. AI models consume
       substantial energy during training and continue to consume energy during inference after
       deployment [2], [8], [9], [22]. For example, the energy consumed during LLaMA 3.1 training
       has been compared to the emissions from 83 years of electricity use by a single household
       in the United States [2]. Prior work has also reported that training a large neural network
       can produce emissions comparable to those of multiple cars over their lifetimes [9]. With
       the increasing demand for large-scale AI systems, data centers are projected to consume a
       growing share of global electricity, reaching approximately 20% by 2030 [9].
           However, in software engineering workflows, sustainability concerns extend beyond model
       training. Once deployed, LLMs consume energy during inference, the process by which
       trained models generate outputs [8], [9], [22]. Inference is particularly relevant in debugging
       and repair because these tasks are often iterative: developers may request multiple fixes,
       regenerate patches, or apply models across many defects. As a result, even moderate
       per-inference costs can accumulate when LLMs are used frequently.
           This concern aligns with Green AI, which argues that AI research should consider
       computational cost alongside model performance [6]. For LLM-based software engineering,
       this means evaluating not only whether a model produces correct outputs, but also the
       inference cost required to produce them. Prior studies have examined the environmental
       impact of large-scale AI systems, the energy efficiency of LLM-generated code, and differences
       between smaller and larger models in programming tasks [2], [9], [16], [20]. These studies
       provide useful evidence, but energy measurements are highly dependent on hardware, task
       configuration, implementation choices, and measurement boundaries [1]. Therefore, results
       from one type of programming task do not necessarily generalize to other software engineering
       activities.
           Bug fixing is a particularly relevant setting for studying correctness-energy trade-offs.
       Developers spend substantial time locating, understanding, and repairing defects [25], [21],
       [23]. Debugging includes several subtasks, such as bug localization, classification, and
       repair, and different bug types may require different levels of reasoning and contextual
       understanding [24]. Python bug fixing is also a practical and challenging target. Python is
       widely used in domains such as data science, machine learning, automation, web development,
       and backend systems. At the same time, Python bugs can be difficult to repair because of
       dynamic typing, heterogeneous data structures, and domain-specific library usage [24].
           Despite growing interest in Green AI and LLM-based software engineering, limited
       empirical evidence is available on the inference-time cost of using open-source code LLMs for
       real-world bug fixing. Much prior work evaluates the efficiency of generated code after it is
       produced, for example, by measuring the runtime or energy consumption of the generated
       program. In contrast, this study focuses on the energy consumed by the model during
       repair generation. This distinction matters because, in LLM-based debugging, sustainability
       concerns arise from both the code eventually produced and the repeated inference required
       to obtain it.

    In this paper, we investigate the correctness–energy trade-offs of LLM-based Python
bug fixing, with particular emphasis on how model size influences both repair effectiveness
and inference cost. We evaluate six open-source code-oriented LLMs, ranging from 1.5B to
15B parameters, on 40 real-world bugs from the BugsInPy benchmark under a consistent
hardware and prompting setup. Our goal is to determine whether increasing model size
improves correctness–cost behavior, or whether smaller models can offer more favorable
trade-offs under the evaluated conditions.
    The study is scoped to localized bug repair, where the faulty code can be isolated to
a single function or class and replaced as a complete block. Repair scenarios that require
repository-level reasoning, coordinated edits across multiple files, or a broader architectural
understanding are outside the scope of this work. Accordingly, the findings and conclusions
should be interpreted in the context of this class of localized repair tasks.
    This paper makes the following contributions:
    We provide a controlled empirical comparison of six open-source code-oriented LLMs on
    real-world Python bug-fixing tasks from BugsInPy.
    We measure repair correctness, inference time, and GPU energy consumption under a
    consistent experimental setup, making inference cost visible alongside functional correct-
    ness.
    We analyze correctness-energy trade-offs across model scales and show that larger models
    do not necessarily provide more efficient LLM-based repair in the evaluated setting.
    We characterize recurring failure patterns in generated repairs to help explain differences
    in model behavior.

    The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses related work on Green
AI, energy-aware LLM evaluation, and LLM-based software repair. Section 3 presents the
research questions and describes the methodology and experimental setup. Section 4 presents
the results. Section 5 discusses implications, and Section 6 presents threats to validity.
Section 7 concludes the paper.

 2     Related Work
Energy-aware software engineering has increasingly examined how software systems, program-
ming practices, and AI-assisted development affect energy consumption. In the context of
LLM-assisted programming, recent work has studied both the energy behavior of generated
code and the computational cost of using the models themselves. This section summarizes
representative studies that motivate our focus on inference-time energy in LLM-based bug
fixing.
    Rani et al. [17] studied whether LLMs can improve the energy efficiency of existing
code. They optimized 400 MATLAB scripts using GPT-3, GPT-4, Llama, and Mixtral,
and compared the results with optimizations produced by a senior MATLAB developer.
Their evaluation considered energy consumption, memory usage, and other performance
metrics. The results showed no clear energy benefit from LLM-based optimization and
indicated negative effects on memory usage. Their findings suggest that LLM-assisted code
optimization does not automatically lead to more sustainable software and that energy effects
must be evaluated empirically.
    A prerequisite for such evaluations is reliable energy measurement. Sallou et al. [18]
addressed this challenge by introducing EnergiBridge, a cross-platform tool for measuring
software energy consumption on Linux, Windows, and macOS. This is relevant because
many earlier energy measurement tools were platform-specific, making comparisons difficult

       across environments. However, even with improved tooling, energy measurements remain
       sensitive to hardware, operating system behavior, workload characteristics, and experimental
       configuration.
           Several studies have evaluated the energy efficiency of code generated by LLMs. Islam
       et al. [9] compared code generated by 20 LLMs with human-written code using energy
       consumption, runtime, memory usage, and correctness. Their results showed that human-
       written code was approximately 1.17 times more energy-efficient than DeepSeek-v3 and
       more efficient than other evaluated LLM outputs. They also found that increasing problem
       complexity was associated with greater inefficiency in LLM-generated code. These findings
       show that functional correctness alone is insufficient for evaluating AI-generated code.
           Ashraf et al. [2] studied energy-aware code generation across model scales. They compared
       three small open-source models, StableCode-3B, StarCoderBase-3B, and Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-
       Instruct, against two large proprietary models, GPT-4.0 and DeepSeek-Reasoner, using 150
       Python programming tasks from LeetCode. Their evaluation measured correctness, runtime,
       memory usage, and energy consumption during execution of the generated code. The results
       showed that large models achieved stronger correctness and runtime performance, while
       small models were more energy-efficient in more than half of the cases where their outputs
       were correct. Their study is closely related to ours because it compares models of different
       sizes under a sustainability lens. However, their measurement target is the execution cost of
       generated code, whereas our study measures the inference cost of generating repairs.
           This distinction is important because energy consumption in AI-assisted software engin-
       eering arises at different points in the workflow. Early Green AI work emphasized the need
       to account for computational costs across the AI life cycle [6, 22]. More recent work shows
       that inference can substantially increase the overall energy footprint of deployed AI systems,
       as models may be executed repeatedly at scale [9]. Fernandez et al. [7] similarly emphasize
       that inference can represent a major share of energy consumption in deployed AI systems.
       In LLM-based debugging, this repeated-use concern is especially relevant: developers or
       tools may request multiple patches, regenerate outputs, or apply models across many defects
       before arriving at a correct fix.
           Overall, prior work provides important evidence on software energy measurement, LLM-
       generated code efficiency, and model-scale comparisons. However, most related studies focus
       on the energy behavior of generated code after execution or on general programming problems.
       Less is known about the inference-time energy cost of using open-source code-oriented LLMs
       for real-world bug-fixing tasks. This study addresses that gap by evaluating six open-source
       code LLMs of different sizes on Python bugs from BugsInPy, measuring repair correctness,
       inference time, and GPU energy consumption during patch generation under a consistent
       experimental setup.


        3     Methodology

       We conduct a controlled experiment to evaluate open-source code-oriented LLMs of different
       sizes on Python bug-fixing tasks. The goal is to examine how model size relates to repair
       correctness, inference time, and GPU energy consumption during repair generation.

   RQ1. How do LLMs of varying sizes differ in their ability to fix Python bugs in terms of
repair correctness and inference time?
   RQ2. What is the inference-time energy cost associated with running these models on
Python bug-fixing tasks?
   RQ3. Which model provides the most favorable trade-off between correctness, inference
time, and energy consumption?
   We investigate two hypotheses:
   H1. Smaller models can achieve comparable repair correctness to larger models while
requiring less inference time and energy consumption.
   H2. Larger models require greater energy resources during inference than smaller models,
regardless of whether the generated fix is correct.



3.2    Experimental Design

For each experimental run, the pipeline takes a selected model and a selected bug as input. It
prepares the buggy project state, extracts the buggy function or class, prompts the model to
generate a repair, inserts the generated repair into the source file, and executes the associated
tests to determine correctness. Figure 1 summarizes this per-run pipeline.
   The study evaluates block-level repair: each generated output replaces the extracted
buggy function or class. This supports automated insertion and testing, but does not evaluate
repository-wide or multi-location repairs.
    Runtime and GPU power are recorded only during LLM inference, from immediately
before repair generation until the model output is returned. Repository setup, extraction,
prompt construction, patch insertion, and test execution are excluded. Because power is
measured using NVML, reported energy values represent GPU-side inference energy, not
full-system energy.
