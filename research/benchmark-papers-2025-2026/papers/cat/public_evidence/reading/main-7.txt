e indirect routes offered to the LLM, which may lower the coverage achieved for some focal methods.
Nevertheless, different LLMs, prompting strategies, or parameter configurations may still yield different results. We therefore encourage future work to further investigate the generalizability of our findings across diverse LLMs, prompt designs, and configuration choices.
External validity.
The main threat to external validity lies in the representativeness of the evaluated projects. To mitigate this concern, we conduct our evaluation on Java projects from Defects4J, a widely adopted benchmark in prior related research, as well as additional GitHub projects that have not been seen by the adopted LLM. Although this setup improves the diversity of the evaluation subjects, the findings may still not fully generalize to projects in other programming languages, domains, or industrial settings.
Our evaluation uses Qwen3-Coder 30B, which was one of the strongest publicly available open-source models for coding tasks when this study was conducted. Different LLMs may respond differently to the same call-chain and dependency contexts. Therefore, the magnitude of the improvements we observe may vary across models, and our results should not be interpreted as model-independent improvements; rather, they support the potential of CAT. More capable models may also infer some project-level information from limited context, potentially reducing the relative improvements provided by CAT. Nevertheless, call-chain and dependency contexts explicitly provide project-specific information about invocation paths and dependencies that may not be available from the focal method alone. In particular, we did not evaluate closed-source models, as doing so would incur substantial additional costs. Prior studies do not report a consistent advantage of closed over open models for unit test generation: Yang et al. (Yang et al., 2024) found that GPT-4 achieved higher line coverage than open models with up to 34B parameters, whereas one of the open models achieved higher branch coverage, and in the evaluation of PANTA (Gu et al., 2025), an open model achieved higher coverage than one closed model and lower coverage than another. Evaluating CAT across different LLM families and capabilities remains an important direction for future work.
Construct validity.
The main threat to construct validity is potential overlap between the LLM’s training data and the evaluated projects, which may inflate performance. To mitigate this issue, we further evaluate our approach on projects that are unlikely to have appeared in the LLM’s training data. As described in Section 4.1.1, we select four Java projects from GitHub that were released after the LLM’s release date and have more than 500 stars. The consistent results on these unseen projects further confirm the effectiveness of our approach, suggesting that the observed performance gains are not solely attributable to memorization.
5. Related Work
5.1. Recent LLM-Based Test Generation with Execution Feedback
Recent advances in LLM-based test generation have demonstrated promising results in producing high-quality unit tests. Representative approaches include PANTA (Gu et al., 2025), SymPrompt (Ryan et al., 2024), and PALM (Wu et al., 2026), the former being the current state of the art. These methods primarily rely on execution-path information to guide LLM-based test generation. However, as discussed in Section 2, path-based guidance alone is often insufficient for complex software systems with rich inter-class dependencies and intricate object initialization requirements.
In contrast, our approach CAT explicitly incorporates call-chain and dependency information into prompt design through static analysis, enabling the LLM to construct valid execution contexts for focal methods in complex projects. We evaluate CAT on both the Defects4J benchmark, used for evaluating existing approaches, and four additional GitHub projects released after the cutoff date of the employed LLM. Experimental results show that CAT consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art path-guided approach (PANTA). We also investigate combining path execution information with our approach, but find that it does not yield improvements across projects (Section 4.2.1), suggesting that naive integration of complementary signals is non-trivial.
5.2. Program-Analysis-Augmented Test Generation
Traditional automated test generation explores the program under test with program analysis rather than LLM reasoning. Random testing, as in Randoop (Pacheco and Ernst, 2007), extends sequences of method calls using execution feedback; search-based testing, as in EvoSuite (Fraser and Arcuri, 2011), evolves test suites towards coverage goals with metaheuristic search; and symbolic execution, as in KLEE (Cadar et al., 2008) and Pex (Tillmann and De Halleux, 2008), derives test inputs by solving path constraints (Cadar and Sen, 2013). These tools construct the objects and arguments needed to reach the code under test themselves, through random or evolved call sequences or through constraint solving, and derive their oracles from observed executions or from generic properties such as crashes. Among them, Randoop and EvoSuite target Java unit tests, the setting of this paper, and EvoSuite serves as the non-LLM baseline of our evaluation (Section 4). LLM-based approaches, including CAT, instead rely on the context in the prompt to construct the required objects and to invoke the focal method, which is the problem that the call-chain and dependency contexts of CAT address.
A line of recent LLM-based approaches also leverages static analysis to provide additional context for test generation. For example, CityWalk (Zhang et al., 2026b) incorporates project-level dependency awareness for C++ test generation. Roychowdhury et al. (Roy Chowdhury et al., 2024) extract method signatures, declaring classes, and field type information to enrich prompts. KAT (Le et al., 2024) constructs operation-level dependency graphs for REST API testing, while RUG (Cheng et al., 2025) uses type dependency graphs for Rust test generation. HITS (Wang et al., 2024) and TELPA (Yang et al., 2026) apply program slicing and contextual analysis to improve coverage of hard-to-reach branches.
PAINT (Nan et al., 2025) explicitly targets branch coverage by performing control-flow graph analysis to extract condition chains that guide the LLM toward executing specific branches. In addition, it trains a machine learning model to predict whether a method call can be replaced with a mock, thereby supporting test construction when direct invocation is not feasible.
ASTER (Pan et al., 2025) adopts a lightweight program analysis strategy to support multi-language test generation. For Java, it focuses on constructing executable test scaffolds by identifying class fields whose types correspond to mockable APIs, and then generating prompts that include constructors, accessor methods (i.e., getters and setters), and method stubs. This design primarily enables object instantiation and external dependency handling via mocking to ensure test executability, rather than explicitly guiding branch exploration.
Importantly, these two approaches operate at different abstraction levels and serve different purposes than CAT: PAINT focuses on branch-level execution guidance, while ASTER focuses on test scaffolding for ensuring executability.
In comparison, CAT performs call-chain extraction and dependency resolution at a more holistic level by integrating call chains, object initialization information, and inter-module dependencies, rather than focusing solely on branch-specific guidance or test scaffolding to ensure executability.
Notably, ASTER and PAINT do not publicly release their implementations, which limits reproducibility and direct comparison in our experiments.
Besides generation-time analysis, another line of work explores using static analysis as a post-processing step. For instance, LLMLOOP (Ravi et al., 2025) and Dolcetti et al. (Dolcetti et al., 2026) employ static analysis to filter invalid tests or guide iterative repair after generation.
A recent survey on LLM-based test generation (Zhang et al., 2026a) highlights that major challenges remain in handling complex software systems, mitigating hallucinations, and improving test validity. It further identifies program analysis, particularly static analysis, as a promising direction for enhancing LLM-based test generation. In this paper, we contribute to that research direction.
In contrast to existing work, CAT focuses on leveraging call-chain and dependency contexts and integrating them into LLM prompts. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to explicitly investigate the role of such context information in project-level LLM-based test generation and demonstrate its effectiveness on both a widely used benchmark and complex real-world software systems.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we presented CAT, an LLM-based test generation approach built on a dedicated static analysis framework that systematically captures execution contexts. By explicitly modeling call-chain and dependency contexts—including caller–callee relationships, object constructors, and third-party dependencies—CAT helps the LLM construct executable and semantically valid contexts for project-level test generation. Our evaluation shows that CAT on average outperforms state-of-the-art approaches on both Defects4J (a widely used benchmark in prior work) and real-world GitHub projects released after the employed LLM’s cut-off date.
We also find that naively combining execution-path information with other signals does not necessarily improve performance, suggesting that effectively integrating complementary sources of information remains challenging. Our ablation study further shows that both call-chain and dependency contexts are essential components of CAT.
Overall, our findings highlight the importance of modeling call-chain-aware execution contexts in LLM-based test generation. We suggest that future work explore more principled methods for integrating heterogeneous signals (e.g., execution paths and input–output relationships) to better support automated test generation. Future work could also improve the precision and completeness of the extracted contexts. For example, propagating receiver types along call chains could prune incompatible dispatch targets, and comparing extracted paths with call edges observed during test execution could quantify how many relevant paths are missed. Since generated tests reside in the focal class’s package, we could admit package-private and protected methods in that package as entry points, and invoke package-private and protected focal methods directly; this would require adapting the visibility filter, the initialization context, and the generation prompt.
In addition, a finer-grained analysis of test fixing across iterations, together with more effective fixing strategies, could improve the recovery of failed generated tests. CAT could also start from an existing test suite (e.g., one generated by EvoSuite) and generate tests only for code left uncovered, potentially reducing the time and cost of test generation. Like the compared approaches, CAT generates regression tests whose assertions reflect current program behavior; combining CAT with oracle generation techniques (Dinella et al., 2022; Jahangirova et al., 2016) could yield tests that also check intended behavior. Finally, evaluating CAT with additional open and closed LLMs, and repeating experiments to measure variance across runs, would clarify the extent to which its improvements depend on the model and on the randomness of LLM sampling.
